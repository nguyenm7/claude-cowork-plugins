# Citations & Source Verification Guide

Find high-confidence, credible sources for research claims, statistics, and quotes.

---

## Source Credibility Tiers

### Tier 1: Gold Standard (Always Cite)
- **Academic research**: Peer-reviewed journals, university studies
- **Industry analysts**: Gartner, Forrester, McKinsey, Bain, HBR
- **Government data**: Census, Bureau of Labor Statistics, regulatory reports
- **Original research**: First-party surveys with clear methodology

**Use for**: Key claims, statistics that anchor an argument

### Tier 2: Strong Sources (Cite with Context)
- **Industry reports**: From recognized research firms
- **Company research**: Published studies from credible companies (not sales material)
- **Expert quotes**: Named individuals with clear credentials
- **Major publications**: WSJ, NYT, Forbes, TechCrunch, The Information

**Use for**: Supporting evidence, trend validation, expert perspectives

### Tier 3: Contextual Sources (Use Carefully)
- **Blog posts**: From recognized thought leaders
- **Podcasts/interviews**: Direct quotes from credible speakers
- **Social media**: Only if from verified expert, and as illustrative example
- **Company blogs**: Good for examples, not for statistics

**Use for**: Color, examples, illustrating a point (not as primary evidence)

### Avoid Entirely
- **Wikipedia** as primary source (can use for background research)
- **Unattributed statistics** ("studies show that...")
- **Competitor marketing materials**
- **Outdated sources** (>3 years for fast-moving topics)
- **Circular citations** (sources that cite each other with no original research)
- **AI-generated content** without verification

## Citation Verification Workflow

### Step 1: Find the Original Source
Many statistics are cited second-hand. Always trace back.

**Red flags**:
- "According to a recent study..." (no link)
- Statistic appears across many sites with same wording
- Link goes to another blog post, not research

**Action**: Search for the original research. If you can't find it, don't use it.

### Step 2: Verify the Claim
Once you find the source, check:

- [ ] **Methodology**: How was data collected? Sample size?
- [ ] **Recency**: When was it published? Still relevant?
- [ ] **Context**: Does the stat mean what you think it means?
- [ ] **Scope**: Does it apply to your audience/market?

### Step 3: Document Properly
For every citation, record:

- Full title of source
- Author/organization
- Publication date
- URL (preferably permalink)
- Specific page/section if applicable
- Date accessed (for web sources)

## Common Statistics & Verified Sources

### Customer Experience Stats

**Bain & Company (often cited)**
> "80% of companies believe they deliver superior customer experience, but only 8% of customers agree."
- **Source**: Bain & Company, "Closing the Delivery Gap"
- **Note**: This is from 2005. Use with "a landmark study found" framing, or find newer data.

**Forrester**
- CX Index reports (annual)
- Customer feedback and VoC research
- Good for enterprise buyer stats

**Qualtrics XM Institute**
- Annual State of CX reports
- Free, well-cited research

### B2B SaaS Stats

**OpenView Partners**
- Product-led growth benchmarks
- SaaS metrics research

**ChartMogul / ProfitWell**
- Churn and retention benchmarks
- Pricing research

**Gainsight / TSIA**
- Customer success benchmarks
- Enterprise SaaS data

### AI & Technology Stats

**Stanford HAI**
- Annual AI Index report
- Credible, comprehensive

**Gartner**
- Magic Quadrants, Hype Cycles
- Technology adoption forecasts

**McKinsey Global Institute**
- AI adoption in enterprise
- Automation impact studies

## How to Cite in Content

### In-Line Citation (Preferred for Blogs)
> According to Bain & Company, 80% of companies believe they deliver superior CX, but only 8% of customers agree.

> A [Forrester study](link) found that companies with strong VoC programs see 10x higher customer retention.

### Parenthetical Citation
> Customer-centric companies are 60% more profitable (Deloitte, 2023).

### Quote Attribution
> "The best product teams treat feedback as a strategic asset, not a support burden."
> — Marty Cagan, *Inspired*

### Data Visualization
When showing stats in graphics:
- Include source in caption or footer
- Format: Source: [Organization], [Year]

## Verification Checklist

Before including any statistic or research claim:

- [ ] **Found original source**: Not a blog citing another blog
- [ ] **Source is credible**: Tier 1 or Tier 2
- [ ] **Claim is accurate**: You read the actual study, not just the headline
- [ ] **Context is preserved**: Stat means what you say it means
- [ ] **Recency is appropriate**: <3 years for most topics, <5 for evergreen
- [ ] **Properly attributed**: Reader can find the original
- [ ] **Link works**: URL is valid and accessible

## When You Can't Find a Good Source

### Option 1: Reframe Without the Stat
**Instead of**: "73% of customers expect personalization"
**Try**: "Customers increasingly expect personalized experiences"

### Option 2: Use Qualitative Evidence
Replace unverifiable stats with:
- Customer quotes (via Wisdom MCP)
- Named company examples
- Industry expert perspectives

### Option 3: Conduct Original Research
If a stat would be valuable and doesn't exist:
- Survey Enterpret customers
- Analyze anonymized platform data
- Partner with a research firm

### Option 4: Cite the Gap
> While precise data is scarce, industry practitioners consistently report that [observation].

## Citation Formatting

### Blog Posts
- Hyperlink the claim to the source
- No formal bibliography needed
- Include "Source: [Name]" under data visualizations

### Whitepapers / Guides
- Include endnotes or inline citations
- Add a References section at the end
- Use consistent formatting (APA or house style)

### LinkedIn / Social
- Mention source by name in post
- Link in comments if needed
- "According to @Forrester..." or "Gartner research shows..."

## Red Flags to Watch For

🚩 **Stat appears everywhere but no one links to original**
→ Likely misquoted or fabricated

🚩 **"Studies show" with no specific study named**
→ Find the study or don't use it

🚩 **Impressive stat from company selling related product**
→ Verify independently or note the conflict

🚩 **Very round numbers (exactly 50%, 10x, etc.)**
→ May be estimates, not research

🚩 **Stat from > 5 years ago in fast-moving space**
→ Find updated data or note the date

🚩 **Source is a content farm or SEO article**
→ Trace to original research
